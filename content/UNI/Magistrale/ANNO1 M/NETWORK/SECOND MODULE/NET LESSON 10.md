##### SLA Monitoring (Service Level Agreement)
before installing a lightpath a client, like an enterprise or an IP operator has to negotiate a Service Level Agreement (SLA) specifying constraints like maximum latency and minimum signal quality. 
Signal quality can be checked at two levels:
- Optical power(optical domain): a rough level physical measurement that checks whether enough light arrives, without inspecting the digital content
- Bit Error Rate, BER (electrical domain): the fraction of received bits that are corrupted. measuring BER requires recovering of the actual binary stream doing an O/E conversion

### Why point-to-point OLTs waste transponders: the 3-node $A$–$B$–$C$ example

Suppose a company wants to connect three regional sites $A$, $B$, and $C$ according to a **logical network** (the optical channels required between site pairs):

- $N_{AB} = 1$ optical channel between $A$ and $B$,
- $N_{BC} = 1$ optical channel between $B$ and $C$,
- $N_{AC} = 3$ optical channels between $A$ and $C$ (higher expected traffic).

In the real world, however, those three sites lie along a single **physical substrate** $A - B - C$ made of two point-to-point fiber links: $A\text{–}B$ and $B\text{–}C$. Because the three $A\text{–}C$ lightpaths have no direct fiber bypassing $B$, both physical spans must carry four wavelengths simultaneously: $1 + 3 = 4$ on $A\text{–}B$, and $1 + 3 = 4$ on $B\text{–}C$.

![[net-l10-abc-logical-vs-physical.svg|820]]
Counting transponder functions across the two architectures makes the waste immediate:

1. **Without OADM — architecture (a) with point-to-point OLTs:**
   Node A uses $4$ transponders, Node C uses $4$, and Node B uses $4 + 4 = 8$ transponders ($4$ to convert every incoming wavelength from A into electricity, and $4$ more to retransmit them toward C):
   $$N_{\mathrm{TP}}^{\mathrm{OLT}} = 4 + 8 + 4 = 16.$$
   Of the $4$ channels arriving at Node B from A, only $1$ is actually meant for B. The other $3$ belong to $A\text{–}C$ and pay an unnecessary $\mathrm{O/E/O}$ conversion twice just to cross B.

2. **With an OADM at Node B — architecture (b):**
   An **Optical Add/Drop Multiplexer (OADM)** keeps the three transit $A\text{–}C$ channels entirely in the optical domain (**optical pass-through**). Node B terminates only the $1$ local $A\text{–}B$ wavelength (**Drop**) and generates the $1$ local $B\text{–}C$ wavelength (**Add**), reducing Node B from $8$ transponders to $2$:
   $$N_{\mathrm{TP}}^{\mathrm{OADM}} = 4 + 2 + 4 = 10.$$
   Replacing point-to-point OLTs at Node B eliminates $6$ transponders—a **37.5% saving** in this example, and approaching a **50% reduction** at intermediate nodes when most traffic is pass-through.
##### OADM how it works
an OADM acts as a 4-port optical device placed along a line or a metropolitan ring interconnecting central offices

![[net-l10-oadm-4port.svg|720]]


Notice an important wavelength-reuse detail in the 4-port view: when the OADM **drops** wavelength $\lambda_1$ (red) to Node B's local client, $\lambda_1$ is removed from the optical signal continuing downstream. Because $\lambda_1$ is now free on the outgoing fiber toward Node C, Node B can **add** its own local $B\text{–}C$ traffic onto that exact same wavelength $\lambda_1$ without interfering with the transit wavelengths ($\lambda_2, \lambda_3, \lambda_4$).

Internally, the professor leaves variants (b), (c), and (d) aside and focuses strictly on **architecture (a)** from Slide 18:

![[net-l10-slide-18-oadm-architectures.png|820]]

In architecture **(a)**, the composite incoming WDM signal ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_W$) enters a **Demux** that separates wavelengths spatially:

- Wavelengths destined locally (here $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$) exit through the **Drop** ports to local receivers.
- Remaining pass-through wavelengths (up through $\lambda_W$) continue straight across to the **Mux** inside the optical domain without electronic conversion.
- Locally originated signals enter through the **Add** ports and combine with the pass-through wavelengths inside a **Mux** onto the outgoing fiber.

Because this basic OADM is **fixed** (physically wired for specific add/drop wavelengths) and sits on a **single incoming/outgoing fiber** (typical of metro rings), it cannot dynamically steer lightpaths across multiple outgoing directions in a meshed backbone—which motivates **Optical Crossconnects (OXCs)** in the next block.
###### Optical Crossconnects(OXCs) how it works with movable mirrors
an OXC we remember that permit the switching optical wavelength channels from incoming links onto outgoing links (other fibers signals)
Unlike an IP router, a purely optical OXC never reads binary packet headers on the fly, because the arriving optical signal stays as light waveforms rather than packets in memory. Instead, switching happens **per wavelength** and is configured when the lightpath is established:
1. **Demultiplexing at entry:** each incoming multi-wavelength fiber first passes through a demultiplexer so that individual wavelengths ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots$) enter the switching fabric separately.
2. **Steering with movable mirrors (MEMS):** inside the switching matrix, electronically controlled **movable mirrors** tilt to redirect a specific incoming wavelength onto a selected outgoing fiber port (for example, bouncing the red wavelength from Input fiber 1 down to Output fiber 2 while other wavelengths pass straight across).
3. **End-to-end mesh provisioning:** to build a lightpath between Client A and Client B across a mesh of OXCs, the network first chooses a route and a wavelength satisfying the continuity constraint, then sets the mirror positions at every traversed OXC along that path. Those mirrors remain fixed for the lifetime of the lightpath rather than changing per packet.
![[net-l10-oxc-mirrors-and-mesh.svg|820]]
oxc functions in a large network:
- service provisioning, automate lightpath setup and teardown accross many nodes without manual fiber patching and reconfiguration
- Protection: detect link or node failures
- Bit-rate and format trasparency: switch optical channels regardless of the client's bit rate or framing format
- Wavelenght conversion: change an incoming signal's wavelength to a different outgoing wavelength whe continuity would otherwise block a path
- Multiplexing and grooming: pack and switch traffic at finer sub-wavelength granularities (we will see it has next topic)
###### Traffic grooming and the muxponder
A single transponder, along with the full capacity of one optical wavelength, is expensive. A huge telecom carrier can fill an entire wavelength on its own, whereas smaller customers (such as small ISPs) generate **low-bit-rate traffic** and cannot afford one dedicated transponder per customer.

**Traffic grooming** solves this by letting multiple small customers share **one transponder** and **one wavelength**:

1. $K$ low-bit-rate customer flows ($f_1, f_2, \dots, f_K$) enter a **TDM multiplexer**, which merges them in the **electrical domain** into one fast digital stream.
2. That combined stream feeds **one shared transponder**, which transmits all $K$ customer flows together on a single wavelength $\lambda$.
3. The combined unit (`TDM Mux + Transponder`) is called a **muxponder**.
4. At the receiving end, separating out one customer's flow $f_i$ requires converting the optical channel back into electricity (`O/E`) and passing it through a **TDM demultiplexer**.

![[net-l10-grooming-muxponder.svg|800]]


TDM Multiplexer+Transponder
- TDM Multiplexer
	- Aggregates mqultiple low-bit-rate streams into one higher-bit-rate stream
- Transponder
	- Adapts the aggregate signal and transmits it on an optical wavelength

If optical pulses become too weak, distorted, or spread out, the receiver may incorrectly interpret a `1` as a `0`, or vice versa.
That increases the Bit Error Rate (BER).
To maintain the quality requested by customers, optical networks use signal regeneration.

###### Types of regeneration
we have 3 levels of it
- 1R Reamplification
	- Restores the optical power
	- An optical amplifier increases the strength of the received signal, but it also amplifies noise.
	- It does not correct distortion or timing errors.
- 2R Reamplification + Reshaping
Restores the amplitude and shape of digital pulses.

It requires O/E/O conversion to recover and reconstruct the signal.

However, the reconstructed pulses may still be shifted in time. This timing variation is called jitter.

Limitation: Timing errors are not corrected.
- 3R Reamplification + Reshaping + Retiming
Restores the amplitude, waveform, and timing.

The regenerator also recovers the clock to resynchronize the digital pulses.

This produces a reconstructed digital signal with corrected timing, assuming the data is still recoverable.


###### Evolution of optical networks
How did optical networks evolve from requiring many expensive 3R regenerators to operating over long distances using mainly optical amplifiers?
###### First generation
Problem A: Multimode fiber

An optical fiber has two main parts: a core, through which light propagates, and surrounding cladding, which helps confine the light within the core.

In early multimode fibers, light could propagate through multiple spatial modes.
