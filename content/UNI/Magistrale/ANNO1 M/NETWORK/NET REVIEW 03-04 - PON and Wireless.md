## Part 1 — Passive Optical Networks (Deck 03)

### 1. Optical Fiber Basics & FTTx
An optical fiber guides light pulses generated by a **laser** (transmitter) and read by a **photodiode** (receiver). We replace copper with fiber in the **access network** to get much higher bit rates over longer distances (tens of km), though digging roads makes deployment cost the main constraint.

* **Attenuation ($\text{dB}$):** Signal power loss as light travels through fiber and splitters.
* **Dispersion:** Light pulses spread out in time as they travel; if they overlap, the receiver cannot tell successive bits apart.
* **Wavelength ($\lambda$, or "color"):** Identifies an optical channel.
  * **Single-mode vs. Multimode** describes how light rays physically propagate inside the glass core.
  * **WDM (Wavelength Division Multiplexing)** means sending **multiple wavelengths ("colors") inside the same fiber at the same time**. *(Note: in the recording the professor casually said "multimode" when talking about multiple colors, and your rough draft notes "larger wavelength = faster bitrate"—don't memorize either for the test. A longer wavelength just has different attenuation windows, not a higher bit rate, and multiple colors is **WDM**, which works on a single-mode fiber).*

**How close does the fiber get? (FTTx)**
* **FTTC (Fiber to the Cabinet/Curb):** Fiber stops at the street cabinet; the final stretch to the house uses existing copper (VDSL). Cheaper, but copper limits speed.
* **FTTB (Fiber to the Building):** Fiber reaches the building basement; copper/Ethernet distributes it inside.
* **FTTH (Fiber to the Home):** Fiber goes all the way into the apartment. Highest performance, highest installation cost.
> **Test note:** If a multiple-choice question asks which has the highest bit rate among **FTTB, FTTC, FTTE, and ADSL** (without listing FTTH), the answer is **FTTB** because fiber gets closest to the user.

### 2. Why PON? Architecture & Splitter Loss
To connect a Central Office to $N$ homes, the professor compares three architectures:
1. **Point-to-Point (Dedicated fibers):** One long fiber per home. Simple, but too many fibers and transceivers.
2. **Active Optical Network (AON):** One shared fiber goes to a **powered electronic switch** in the street, which routes packets to each home. Expensive because the street switch needs electricity, space, and maintenance.
3. **Passive Optical Network (PON):** One shared fiber goes to an **unpowered optical splitter** (fused glass) in the street. Only the endpoints (**OLT** at the provider and **ONU/ONT** at the user side) need electricity.

![[net-review-0304-pon.svg]]

* **OLT (Optical Line Terminal):** Provider-side device at the Central Office. Controls the network.
* **ONU / ONT (Optical Network Unit / Terminal):** User-side box that converts between optical fiber and home Ethernet/Wi-Fi. *(It is an optical termination unit, not a core IP router).*
* **Passive Splitter:** Passively divides light going to homes and merges light coming back.
  * Every time a $1:2$ splitter divides light equally, each branch gets half the power ($\approx \mathbf{3\text{ dB}}$ **loss**).
  * For an ideal $1:N$ split, each branch receives $1/N$ of the power:
    $$L_{\text{split}} = 10\log_{10}(N)\text{ dB}$$
    *(e.g., a $1:8$ split cascades three $1:2$ splits $\rightarrow 3 + 3 + 3 \approx 9\text{ dB}$ loss).*

> [!important] Key Test Takeaway — Why use a PON?
> The main advantage of a PON over an active optical network is **lower infrastructure and operating cost** (no powered switch in the street). You still need ONUs at the homes, and you still need upstream scheduling.

### 3. Downstream vs. Upstream in PON (and EPON)
A single fiber carries both directions at once by using **two different wavelengths** (one "color" for downstream, another for upstream).

| Direction | How it works | Main challenge & solution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Downstream** (OLT $\rightarrow$ ONUs) | **1 sender, many receivers (TDM Broadcast).** The OLT sends one stream of frames; the splitter copies that light to *every* ONU. Each ONU keeps only frames with its own destination address. | **Privacy / Security:** Because every ONU physically receives everyone else's downstream light, a malicious user could eavesdrop or infer traffic patterns (who is active, how much data they receive) unless traffic is encrypted. |
| **Upstream** (ONUs $\rightarrow$ OLT) | **Many senders, 1 receiver (TDMA).** If two ONUs transmit at the same time on the upstream wavelength, their light pulses **collide** at the splitter. | **No carrier sensing:** An ONU cannot "listen" to see if another ONU is transmitting because upstream light travels only toward the OLT. **Solution:** The **OLT schedules exact time slots** for each ONU, separated by short **guard intervals**. |

* **Why does the OLT need Automatic Gain Control (AGC)?**
  ONUs are at different distances from the OLT, so one ONU's upstream burst may arrive strong while the next ONU's burst arrives weak. A **burst-mode receiver with AGC** rapidly adjusts the OLT's receiver gain/threshold at the start of every single burst.
  * *Don't mix them up:* **OLT time scheduling** prevents upstream collisions; **AGC** handles different received power levels.
* **What is EPON (Ethernet PON)?**
  It simply encapsulates standard **Ethernet frames** over a PON physical layer (using OLT scheduling, not old Ethernet collision detection).

### 4. WDM-PON: Separating Users by Wavelength
Instead of making all ONUs share the same wavelength in different time slots (TDMA), **WDM-PON (WDMA)** gives each ONU its own dedicated wavelength ("color") pair.
* All wavelengths travel together down the same feeder fiber.
* Instead of a simple power splitter, a passive wavelength-selective device (**AWG — Arrayed Waveguide Grating**, sometimes informally called an "optical router") directs each specific wavelength to its matching ONU branch.
* **Advantage:** Users transmit simultaneously without sharing time slots. **Limitation:** More expensive wavelength-specific optical components, and the number of supported users is limited by the available wavelengths.
* **Test trap:** "10 wavelengths in WDM" means **10 optical channels ("colors") in one fiber**, *not* 10 separate fibers.

## Part 2 — Wireless & Cellular Infrastructures (Deck 04)

### 5. Wireless Basics: Infrastructure vs. Ad Hoc Mode
* **Wireless Host / UE (User Equipment):** Any end device using the network (phone, laptop, IoT sensor). *Note from your draft: "UE" is the 4G/5G cellular term for any subscriber terminal, whether it is moving or sitting on a desk.*
* **Base Station:** Connects wireless devices to the wired core network (**AP — Access Point** in Wi-Fi; **eNodeB** in 4G LTE). Both the base station and the user terminal must have enough power/sensitivity to hear each other (a huge tower power is useless if the phone's weak reply cannot reach the tower).

| Mode | How it works | Pros & Cons (Exam Topic!) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Infrastructure Mode** | All devices talk through a Base Station / AP connected to the wired core. Moving between base stations is called a **handover (handoff)**. | **Pros:** Centralized control, easy Internet access, managed mobility/security.<br>**Cons:** High infrastructure deployment cost; relies on base station coverage. |
| **Ad Hoc Mode (MANET / VANET)** | Devices talk directly peer-to-peer (single-hop or **multihop**, where nodes route packets for each other). **VANET** = Vehicular Ad Hoc Network. | **Pros:** No base station needed; fast direct local communication (e.g., car-to-car braking alerts).<br>**Cons:** Routing is very hard when nodes move constantly; nodes must trust each other (malicious nodes could drop/spy on traffic); battery drain from forwarding. |

### 6. The 3 Wireless Link Impairments & SNR
The professor explicitly highlighted the **three physical problems of a wireless link** as an exam question:

1. **Decreased Signal Strength (Path Loss):** Electromagnetic waves weaken as they spread over distance and pass through walls/obstacles. Higher frequencies generally attenuate faster over distance.
2. **Interference from Other Sources:** Other transmitters using the **same frequency band at the same time** corrupt the signal at the receiver (e.g., Wi-Fi and Bluetooth both on 2.4 GHz, or multiple users transmitting together).
   * *Test distinction:* When multiple users share a wireless network, the main challenge they create for each other is **interference** (requiring multiple-access coordination).
3. **Multipath Propagation:** A single transmitted signal reaches the receiver multiple times via different paths—first via the direct path, and fractions of a second later via reflections off walls, buildings, and the ground.
   * Because the reflected copies arrive with different delays and phases, they can blur symbols or cancel each other out (**destructive self-interference**).
   * *Test distinction:* Multipath happens even with a **single transmitter**, and to compensate for or exploit it constructively, the receiver must estimate the **attenuations and delays of the different paths** (knowing only the direct path delay is not enough).

**SNR, BER, and Rate Adaptation**
$$\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{signal}}}{P_{\mathrm{noise}}}, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}(\mathrm{SNR})$$
* **BER (Bit Error Rate):** Probability of receiving a corrupted bit.
* **How they connect:** A cleaner channel (higher **SNR**) gives a lower **BER**, allowing the sender to switch to a denser modulation (more bits per symbol $\rightarrow$ **higher bit rate**). If you walk far from the base station and SNR drops, the system switches to a slower, more robust modulation so BER stays acceptable. Users report their channel quality via a **CQI (Channel Quality Indicator)**.

### 7. Multiple Access: TDMA, FDMA, CDMA
How do multiple wireless users share the air without destroying each other's signals? (The professor asked only for the high-level intuition):

* **TDMA (Time Division):** Users take turns in different time slots on the same frequency.
* **FDMA (Frequency Division):** Users transmit in separate frequency sub-bands.
* **CDMA (Code Division):** Users transmit at the **same time and on the same frequency**, without colliding, because each user multiplies every data bit $d \in \{-1, +1\}$ by a unique orthogonal **chipping code** $c_m \in \{-1, +1\}$ (your draft wrote *"cheap code"*—the real spelling is **chip / chipping sequence**!):
  $$z_m = d \cdot c_m \quad (\text{sender}), \qquad \hat{d} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} r_m c_m \quad (\text{receiver})$$
  When the receiver multiplies the combined air signal $r_m$ by user A's code and averages over $M$ chips, user A's bit pops out while other users' orthogonal codes cancel to zero.
* **Modern Cellular (4G/5G Time-Frequency Grid):** Combines **TDMA + FDMA** into a grid of time-frequency slots. To give a user a higher bit rate, the scheduler simply assigns them more slots in the grid.

### 8. Wi-Fi (802.11): Scanning, Hidden Terminal, CSMA/CA & RTS/CTS

#### A. Finding an AP: Passive vs. Active Scanning
* **Passive scanning:** The Wi-Fi client stays quiet and listens across channels for periodic **Beacon frames** broadcast by APs (which announce the network's **SSID**), then sends an Association Request.
* **Active scanning:** The client actively broadcasts a **Probe Request**, waits for nearby APs to reply with a **Probe Response**, and then associates.

#### B. The Hidden Terminal Problem
Suppose **A** and **C** both want to send data to **B** (the AP), but an obstacle or distance prevents **A** and **C** from hearing each other.
* **A** listens to the air, hears nothing, and starts sending to **B**.
* **C** also listens, cannot hear **A**, thinks the air is free, and sends to **B** at the same time $\rightarrow$ **collision at B!**

![[net-review-0304-hidden-rts.svg]]

#### C. How Wi-Fi Avoids Collisions: CSMA/CA vs. RTS/CTS
Wi-Fi uses **CSMA/CA (Collision Avoidance)**—*not* CSMA/CD (Collision Detection), because a wireless antenna cannot easily hear weak distant collisions while it is loudly transmitting its own signal.

1. **Basic CSMA/CA (with DIFS and SIFS):**
   * Before sending, the sender listens. If the channel is free for **DIFS**, it sends the DATA frame (if busy, it waits and counts down a random backoff timer whenever the channel is idle).
   * When receiver **B** gets the frame intact, **B** waits a very short time called **SIFS** and sends an **ACK**.
   * **Why is $\text{SIFS} < \text{DIFS}$?** Because SIFS is shorter than DIFS, the receiver's ACK goes out *before* any other waiting device (which must wait a full DIFS) can grab the channel. If the sender gets no ACK, it assumes a failure, doubles its random backoff window, and retries.
2. **Optional RTS/CTS Reservation (Solving your note doubt!):**
   * *Your note asked: "Why should C receive something if A wants to send to B?"*
   * Because **C cannot hear A**, **B** must be the one to warn **C** to stay quiet!
   * **Step 1:** **A** sends a tiny **RTS (Request to Send)** frame to **B** saying how long the transmission will take.
   * **Step 2:** **B** broadcasts a tiny **CTS (Clear to Send)** frame. **Both A and C hear B's CTS!**
   * **Step 3:** For **A**, the CTS means *"Go ahead and send DATA."* For **C**, that exact same CTS means *"Someone else is talking to B for $X$ milliseconds—stay quiet and save your battery/time!"*
   * **Trade-off:** RTS/CTS adds extra overhead (two extra frames + SIFS waits), so it isn't used for every tiny packet—it is useful when frames are large or hidden terminals cause frequent collisions.

### 9. 4G LTE Architecture, Tunneling, Sleep & 5G
4G LTE is an **all-IP network** (both voice and data travel as IP packets). It separates the **Control Plane** (signalling, authentication, mobility setup) from the **Data Plane** (actual user packets like video/web).

![[net-review-0304-lte-tunnel.svg]]

* **Data Plane path:** $\text{UE} \leftrightarrow \text{eNodeB (Base Station)} \leftrightarrow \mathbf{S\text{-}GW}\text{ (Serving Gateway)} \leftrightarrow \mathbf{P\text{-}GW}\text{ (Packet Data Network Gateway)} \leftrightarrow \text{Internet}$.
* **Control Plane brains:**
  * **HSS (Home Subscriber Server):** The home operator's central database storing your SIM profile and subscription rights.
  * **MME (Mobility Management Entity):** The control server that authenticates your phone (by checking the HSS), tracks which cells you are near, and sets up tunnels when you move. *(User video packets never pass through the MME or HSS!)*

#### Resolving your note doubt: How does LTE Tunneling (GTP-U, slide 52) work?
*Your note asked about "mapping" and putting an IP packet inside another IP packet.*
Suppose your phone has IP address `10.1.1.5` and you are watching a video while on a train. As the train moves from **eNodeB 1** to **eNodeB 2**, you don't want your phone's IP address to change (otherwise your video connection would drop), and you can't force every router on the Internet to update its routing table every time your train moves!
* **The Tunneling solution:** The **P-GW** (or **S-GW**) takes your original IP packet (`Hip + Pip`) and wraps it inside a **new outer IP packet** (using UDP + the **GTP-U** header) addressed to the IP address of the **current eNodeB**:
  $$\underbrace{\text{Outer IP (to eNodeB)} \mid \text{UDP} \mid \text{GTP-U}}_{\text{Tunnel envelope}} \;\Big|\; \underbrace{\text{Original User IP Packet}}_{\text{Your unchanged IP packet}}$$
* Normal routers inside the operator core only look at the **Outer IP header** to deliver the packet to the current eNodeB. When you move to a new eNodeB (handover), the MME simply updates the **mapping** at the gateway so the Outer IP points to the *new* eNodeB.

#### Association, Sleep Modes & What Changes in 5G
* **Association:** Base stations continuously broadcast synchronization signals; your phone scans for them and connects (using the strongest permitted operator signal), then sets up a data session.
* **Sleep modes (Battery saving):** Continuously scanning and listening drains the battery, so LTE uses **Light Sleep** (wakes up quickly after a few hundred ms of inactivity) and **Deep Sleep** (after ~5–10 seconds of inactivity on the slide; saves more battery, but takes longer to wake up and reconnect).
* **5G Evolution (Two main points from the lecture):**
  1. **Millimeter Wave (mmWave) frequencies:** Much higher frequencies offer huge bandwidth (very high bit rates), but signals travel very short distances and are easily blocked by walls/obstacles $\rightarrow$ requires a dense network of small cells and directional beams (alongside lower bands).
  2. **Flexible / Edge Architecture & Low Latency:** Instead of forcing all traffic through a distant central gateway, 5G virtualizes network functions and places them closer to the user (at the network edge).
  * **Test distinction (Bit rate vs. Latency):** Why does 5G care so much about **latency**? Because interactive/safety-critical applications (like self-driving cars or remote control) require immediate **responsiveness** (short reaction time), which a high bit rate (throughput) alone cannot guarantee.

### 10. Mobility: Home vs. Visited Network (Indirect vs. Direct Routing)
When you travel abroad (roaming), your **Home Network** (e.g., in Rome, holding your HSS and permanent home address) is separate from the **Visited Network** (e.g., in Berlin, where your phone is currently attached).

![[net-review-0304-mobility.svg]]

How do packets from a Correspondent (e.g., a server or another person in Berlin) reach you?

| Method | How packets travel | Pros & Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Indirect Routing** | Correspondent sends packets to your **Home Network** (Rome), which tunnels them to the **Visited Network** (Berlin) and to you. | **Pros:** Completely transparent to the correspondent; easy to update if you move to another visited network.<br>**Cons:** **Triangle routing**—even if both of you are standing in Berlin, packets take a long detour through Rome! |
| **Direct Routing** | Correspondent first queries the Home Network to learn your Berlin visited address, then sends packets **directly** to Berlin. | **Pros:** No detour through Rome (low delay).<br>**Cons:** Correspondent must handle mobility updates if you move again; **privacy issue** because the correspondent learns which visited network/location you are in. |

* **Handover between Base Stations (within a network):** When signal measurements show a target eNodeB is better than the current source eNodeB, the source eNodeB coordinates with the target, temporarily forwards in-flight packets to the target so nothing is lost, and the MME switches the S-GW tunnel to the target eNodeB.

### Quick Trap-Buster Table
| If a test question asks about... | Remember this key distinction |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Main advantage of PON vs. Active Optical Network** | **Lower infrastructure cost** (passive glass splitter in the field needs no power). |
| **Preventing upstream collisions in PON** | **OLT-controlled time scheduling (TDMA)** with guard intervals—*not* carrier sensing at ONUs and *not* power adjustment. |
| **Burst-mode AGC at the OLT** | Adapts receiver gain/threshold because different ONUs arrive at different power levels (does *not* prevent collisions). |
| **"10 wavelengths" in WDM** | Means **10 optical channels ("colors") inside a single fiber**, not 10 fibers. |
| **Best bit rate among FTTB, FTTC, FTTE, ADSL** | **FTTB** (fiber gets closest to the user among those four choices). |
| **Main problem caused by multiple wireless users** | **Interference** at the receiver (requires multiple access: TDMA/FDMA/CDMA/CSMA). |
| **Exploiting / fixing Multipath propagation** | Requires knowing the **attenuations and delays of the different reflected paths** (not just the direct path). Occurs even with 1 sender. |
| **Why C stays quiet in RTS/CTS (Hidden Terminal)** | C cannot hear A's RTS, but **C hears B's CTS**, which reserves the channel for A's transmission duration. |
| **Why SIFS is shorter than DIFS** | Gives ongoing replies (**ACK / CTS**) priority over new senders waiting for DIFS. Failed sends double the **contention window**, not DIFS. |
| **Why 5G / interactive apps need low latency** | For **responsiveness** (fast reaction time), which high bit rate/throughput alone does not provide. |
| **LTE MME/HSS vs. S-GW/P-GW** | **MME & HSS** are Control Plane (auth, subscriber DB, tunnel setup); **S-GW & P-GW** carry Data Plane user packets via **GTP-U tunnels**. |

> [!example]- Click to check your answers
> 1. **PON Cost & Splitter:** The field splitter is passive fused glass (no electricity or electronic switch in the street). A $1:2$ split divides optical power in half ($\approx 3\text{ dB}$ loss per branch).
> 2. **Downstream vs. Upstream:** Downstream is broadcast by the OLT to all branches (so any ONU could read other users' frames if unencrypted). Upstream merges signals from independent ONUs that cannot hear each other, so without OLT time-slot scheduling their light pulses collide.
> 3. **Scheduling vs. AGC:** OLT scheduling assigns separate time slots so upstream bursts don't overlap in time. AGC rapidly adjusts the OLT receiver's sensitivity for each burst because near and far ONUs arrive with different optical power levels.
> 4. **WDM vs. Multimode:** WDM puts multiple wavelengths ("colors"/channels) into the same fiber simultaneously. Single-mode/multimode refers to the physical geometry of light paths inside the glass core.
> 5. **Infrastructure vs. Ad Hoc:** Infrastructure uses an AP/base station (easy management and Internet access, but costs money to deploy). Ad hoc connects nodes directly/multihop (great for fast local links like VANETs without towers, but routing moving nodes and trusting peers is hard).
> 6. **Multipath:** One signal arrives multiple times via the direct path plus reflections off walls/objects. The receiver must estimate the **attenuations and delays of each path**.
> 7. **CDMA:** Each user multiplies their bits by a unique orthogonal **chipping code** ($c_m \in \{-1,+1\}$). The receiver correlates the signal with the desired user's code, cancelling out other users.
> 8. **RTS/CTS:** C is hidden from A and misses A's RTS, but C hears B's CTS and defers for the announced duration. It's worth the overhead for long data frames or heavy hidden-terminal collisions.
> 9. **GTP-U Tunneling:** The gateway wraps the user's unchanged IP packet inside an outer IP/UDP/GTP-U packet addressed to the current eNodeB. On handover, the core just updates the outer eNodeB address.
> 10. **Indirect vs. Direct Routing:** Indirect routing sends packets all the way to the user's Home Network first (triangle routing detour). Direct routing sends straight to Berlin, but complicates mobility updates if the user moves again and exposes the user's visited location to the correspondent.
