### Recursive Customer-Provider Relationships
In the networks we have two principal role
- customers, who use the services
- providers, who give the services

they can both be ISP (Internet Service Providers) because they can give services to each other or maybe one gives services to a customer, and the customer instead gives services to users
we have two different relationships
in a better form i can say that 
The customer–provider relationship is recursive because the same entity can act as both a provider and a customer at the same time.

###### Horizontal relationship
We have access providers operating in different geographical areas, such as Rome and Milan. To establish communication between their users, they can rely on a transport network managed by another provider, purchasing connectivity services from it.
The relationship is **horizontal** because it occurs between different network domains, and **recursive** because the same entity can act as both a customer and a provider.
![[download.svg]]
###### Vertical relationship
In a vertical relationship, we focus on different network layers rather than different geographical or administrative domains.
For example, an IP network (L3) can request a logical point-to-point connection between two routers from an underlying optical network (L1), which provides the physical connectivity.
The upper layer acts as the customer, while the lower layer acts as the provider. The customer does not need to know how the underlying network implements the requested service.

##### Traffic aggregation concept
An access network collects many individual traffic by the users called traffic flows, it has to combine them into an aggregated traffic demand
(maybe here i can put the picture cited before)

The aggregate flow can be represented as:

\[ F_{\text{agg}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_i \]

However, users do not continuously transmit at their maximum available bit rate. Their traffic is typically **bursty**.

we can have more FTTH subscribers that have:
- nominal capacity
- average traffic used

so we can also make an average of the aggregated traffic sum and not of the nominal ones
1,000 subscribers with a nominal capacity of 1 Gbps generate an average aggregate demand of only 50 Gbps, assuming an average of 50 Mbps per subscriber.

so providers can use statistical multiplexing for the traffic without allocating the sum of the nominal traffic demand

This is why providers can exploit **statistical multiplexing** instead of allocating the sum of all maximum access capacities.

##### Network Topologies and the importance of Routing
A network can be designed with different topologies
![[net-l08-topologies.svg|900]]
in a point to point or a tree one the Central Office is forced to be in a single point, so the path is only one, this create difficulty for shared networks causing congestions or fails because there arent alternative paths
Congestion occurs when incoming traffic demand exceeds the available capacity, resulting in increased queuing delay and eventually packet loss when buffers fill.

the ring can fix this problem using the opposite directions but they are only two

in a mesh topology we can have different path using different routing functions
- routing is like a function that decide the path

|Function|Purpose|
|---|---|
|**Service delivery**|Select a suitable path between endpoints.|
|**Service restoration**|Find alternative paths following a failure.|
|**Load balancing**|Distribute traffic to avoid congestion and improve resource utilization.|

##### Logical vs Physical Network
because building transport fiber infrastructures is expensive usually ISPs use the same physical one but offering a logical different connection
The clients only see their logical connections, while the transport provider manages the actual fibers, optical devices and physical routes.
infact the Transport networks have two important characteristics:

>[!question]- is TCP connection-oriented?
>TCP is connection-oriented with a point of view of the transport layer but in the network one it remains connectionless

An important thing to say is that optical networks are connection-oriented
that's because doing the estabilish of the connection introduces an initial overhead. the network must compute a path, allocate resources and configure its devices

in case of physical optical connections, they usually are configured to be active for months or years, a connection from Rome to Milan of an ISP for example
The reason optical networks are connection-oriented is that **the cost of configuring the underlying infrastructure is negligible compared to the long lifetime of the requested connection.**
the effective estabilished end-to-end optical connection is called a ***lightpath***

###### The lightpath lifecycle
- 1) connection request
	- the customer specifies the endpoints and service requirements such as bandwidth
- 2) Connection steup
	- the provider selects a path, allocates optical channels ad configures intermediate switching devices
- 3) Traffic exchange and maintenance
	- The consumer uses the connection while the provider monitors its performance and detects failures
- 4) Connection teardown
	- The connection is removed and its resources become available to other customers
Notice that this is another example of the vertical customer–provider relationship: the IP network requests connectivity, while the optical network establishes the lightpath.
##### Static vs statistical multiplexing
Multiplexing is used in a connection for allowing multiple traffic flows in a common transmission medium

With **static multiplexing**, resources are allocated to a specific connection in advance. If the customer is not transmitting, those resources remain unused.
With **statistical multiplexing**, resources are dynamically shared among active traffic flows, improving resource utilization but potentially introducing congestion and queuing delays.
